Converting a 1984 Honda XR250R into an electric dirt bike

I'm rebuilding a $100 rolling chassis around a 72-volt mid-drive motor and a battery pack I'm building myself. The targets: 40 mph, 20 miles per charge, under $1,500, and done within one school year.

Build progress

    Design decisions

    What I chose, why, and what I ruled out.

    Frame: a full-size steel XR250R

    Chose a mid-1980s Honda XR250R rolling chassis (frame, Pro-Link swingarm, wheels, forks, no engine) for $100. Its frame plate says it was built in October 1983 as a 1984 model.

    Why: a full-size steel frame has the geometry and strength for EV torque and battery weight, and steel is easy to weld new motor mounts and a battery box onto.

    Ruled out: mini bikes like the YZ80 and KX80, and the DR100, whose thinner tubing and forks aren't built for this load.

    Known weak points I'm inspecting: steering head bearings, swingarm pivot bushings, cracking at the Pro-Link mount and steering head gusset, subframe fatigue, the drum brake and the soft damper-rod forks.

    Drive: mid-drive, not a hub motor

    Chose a mid-drive motor that sits where the engine was and drives the stock chain. That keeps the original swingarm, and the gearing multiplies torque for hill climbing.

    Target motor: QS138 70H V3, 3,000 W continuous and 5,500 to 6,000 W peak at 72 V, with an EM150-2SP controller (a Fardriver ND72680 is the alternative). It's sold as a kit for dirt bike conversions for about $615.

    Trade-off: 3,000 W continuous is right at, or a little under, the roughly 3.5 kW I estimate is needed to hold 40 mph. Peak power covers hills and acceleration, but long top-speed runs may make the motor run warm. The larger QS138 90H or QS165 would add headroom, so I'm still weighing them.

    Battery chemistry: lithium-ion 18650 cells

    Chose lithium-ion 18650 cells in a 20-series pack for 72 V nominal.

    Ruled out: LiFePO4. It has better cycle life and is safer, but reaching the same voltage takes 23 cells in series instead of 20 and adds a lot of weight, which is a poor fit for a dirt bike.

    Battery management: a JBD 20S smart BMS (60 A or 80 A, with Bluetooth), chosen over Daly.

    Cell source: salvaged, but only if they test well

    Plan A was 140 new name-brand cells (Samsung 30Q or LG MJ1 class) from verified sellers like 18650batterystore, Fogstar and Illumn. Cheap marketplace listings often sell counterfeit cells with fake brand names.

    Now: I have a salvaged Tesla battery pack with one blown section. If its good cells pass testing, they could cover the pack for a fraction of the cost.

    Before I trust them: confirm the exact cell model and datasheet, make sure each cell can handle about 6 A continuous, and capacity- and load-test a sample batch. Building the pack is the highest-risk phase, so it's done with my mentor.

    Sizing the battery

    How the range and speed targets turn into a pack of 140 cells.

    1. Voltage72 V motor ÷ 3.6 V per cell20 cells in series (20S)
    2. Energy20 miles × 75 Wh per mile1,500 Wh minimum
    3. Capacity1,500 Wh ÷ 72 V ≈ 21 Ah, plus a buffer24.5 Ah target
    4. Parallel groups24.5 Ah ÷ 3.5 Ah per cell7 in parallel: 20S7P, 140 cells
    5. Peak current6,000 W rated peak ÷ 72 V ≈ 83 A, split across 7 cells~12 A per cell
    6. Continuous current3,000 W ÷ 72 V ≈ 42 A, split across 7 cells~6 A per cell
    7. Weight140 cells, before the BMS, box and wiring~15 lb

    The EM150 controller can draw up to 150 A from the battery unless its current limit is set lower, which would be over 20 A per cell in a 7P pack. Its battery-current limit has to be set to match the cells and the BMS.

    75 Wh per mile is a blended estimate for mixed easy and rough terrain, based on similar electric dirt bikes. Gentle riding should beat 20 miles; hard riding on rough ground could drop it to 15 to 17.
